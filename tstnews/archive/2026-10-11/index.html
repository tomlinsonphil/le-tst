Iran war and the Strait of Hormuz

Not verified today

The Iran conflict and the risk to shipping around the Strait of Hormuz were still running as live stories this week, but I could not confirm today's front-line position or the latest diplomatic moves.

For a reader outside the region the practical question is how it feeds into oil prices, and from there into inflation, interest rates and the stock and bond markets you may hold through ETFs.

If you're new to this

The Strait of Hormuz is a narrow stretch of sea between Iran to the north and Oman to the south, linking the Persian Gulf to the open ocean. Roughly a fifth of the world's oil, and a large share of its liquefied natural gas, passes through it. There is no easy alternative route for most of that cargo.

That is why any threat to ships there makes traders nervous. Even without a closure, higher insurance costs and rerouted tankers push the price of oil up. Oil is an input to almost everything: petrol, flights, plastics, fertiliser and freight. When it rises, general inflation tends to rise too.

Inflation matters because central banks such as the Bank of Canada, the Bank of England and the US Federal Reserve respond to it by moving interest rates. Higher rates make borrowing dearer and usually weigh on bond prices and on shares, especially in growth sectors.

So a conflict thousands of kilometres away reaches a Toronto investor through fuel costs, mortgage rates and the value of an ETF portfolio.

Ukraine war

Not verified today

The war continues. My searches did not confirm today's battlefield or negotiating position, so treat this as a placeholder to refresh.

The war's wider effects are clearer than its daily moves: high European defence spending, energy-market sensitivity and sanctions on Russia all show up in national budgets, including France's and the UK's.

If you're new to this

In February 2022 Russia launched a full-scale invasion of Ukraine, which had been fighting Russian-backed forces in the east since 2014. Ukraine's Western backers have supplied weapons, money and training, and imposed sanctions on Russia.

Europe feels the war economically because Russia had been a major supplier of gas. Countries rebuilt their energy supplies, and higher costs fed into the inflation spike of 2022 to 2023.

The war also changed government spending. NATO members have pledged to spend more on defence, which squeezes budgets already stretched by ageing populations and high debt.

When you read about a ceasefire or talks, the markets usually watch three things: energy prices, defence-company shares, and the borrowing costs of European governments.

Election · 26 Oct

Toronto votes for its mayor and city councillors on 26 October. The contest is reported as a two-way race between the incumbent, Olivia Chow, and Brad Bradford, the Beaches-East York councillor.

The campaign is likely to turn on housing and affordability, transit, public safety and how the city pays for its services.

  • Election day: 26 October 2026
  • Candidates reported: Olivia Chow (incumbent) and Brad Bradford
  • Also on the ballot: councillors for the city's wards, and school board trustees

If you're new to this

Toronto's mayor leads a council of 25 ward councillors plus the mayor. The mayor chairs council meetings, proposes the annual budget and represents the city to other governments. Since 2022 Ontario has also given the mayors of Toronto and Ottawa "strong mayor" powers, which let them propose budgets and hire senior staff with fewer council votes.

Much of what you notice as a resident sits with the city: property tax, garbage collection, the TTC, road maintenance, parks, libraries, shelters and zoning. Big items such as health care and the main income tax sit with the province and the federal government.

You vote only in your own ward for a councillor, but everyone votes for the mayor. To vote, you need to be on the voters list, which you can check through the city's election website, and you will get a voter notification card. Advance voting is usually offered before election day.

Why it matters beyond politics: the city's choices on development, transit funding and property tax shape rents, commute times and house prices, which feed into the wider economy.

London: Burnham government and the Bank of England

Westminster

Andy Burnham has been Prime Minister since 20 July, with John Healey as Chancellor of the Exchequer. The pair now face the usual autumn squeeze between promises on spending and a tight fiscal position.

On the money side, the Bank of England held its Bank Rate at 3.75% on 17 September. UK consumer price inflation is running at 3.1%, above the bank's 2% target, which is why rate cuts have not come faster. The next rate decision is on 5 November.

  • Bank Rate: 3.75% (held 17 Sep; next decision 5 Nov)
  • CPI inflation: 3.1% against a 2% target
  • Prime Minister: Andy Burnham (since 20 July)
  • Chancellor: John Healey

If you're new to this

The UK has a parliamentary system. The leader of the party that can command a majority in the House of Commons becomes Prime Minister. The Chancellor of the Exchequer is the finance minister, who sets taxes and the government's spending plans, usually announced in a Budget in the autumn.

The Bank of England is independent of the government. A nine-member Monetary Policy Committee sets Bank Rate, meeting eight times a year. Its job is to keep CPI inflation at 2%. If inflation is above the target, it holds rates high or raises them to cool spending. If it is below the target and the economy is weak, it cuts.

Bank Rate matters to ordinary people because mortgages, savings accounts and business loans follow it. It also influences gilt yields. Gilts are UK government bonds. When investors expect higher rates or worry about government borrowing, gilt yields rise and prices fall.

For a Canadian-based ETF investor the UK matters mainly through the pound against the Canadian dollar and through any UK shares inside a global fund.

Paris: the 2027 budget

Politics and money

Prime Minister Sébastien Lecornu presented the 2027 budget on 1 October. It aims to cut the state deficit from 5.4% of GDP this year to 5% next year. Public debt is about 121.7% of GDP, and France's 10-year government bond yield is near 4.94%.

Budgets are hard to pass in France because no party holds a clear majority in the National Assembly. Expect weeks of negotiation and the risk of a confidence vote.

  • 2027 budget presented: 1 October
  • Deficit target: 5.4% of GDP down to 5%
  • Public debt: about 121.7% of GDP
  • 10-year OAT yield: about 4.94%

If you're new to this

A deficit is the gap between what a government spends and what it collects in a year. Debt is the running total of past deficits. The EU's rules ask members to keep deficits below 3% of GDP and debt below 60%, so France is well outside both.

France's president picks a prime minister, who runs the government and must have enough support in the National Assembly. To pass a budget without a vote, the government can use a constitutional provision known as Article 49.3. That lets it adopt the text unless a no-confidence motion succeeds, and a successful motion topples the government.

The OAT (Obligation assimilable du Trésor) is the French government bond. When investors worry about France's finances they demand a higher yield. Many people compare the OAT yield with Germany's Bund. A widening gap is a quick gauge of political stress.

For an investor, French politics matters because a budget crisis can hit French banks and shares, and the euro.

Sablet

If you're new to this

Sablet is a hilltop village in the Vaucluse, in the Provence-Alpes-Côte d'Azur region, below the jagged limestone ridge of the Dentelles de Montmirail. It is part of the Côtes du Rhône Villages appellation and is one of the villages allowed to put its own name on the label.

An appellation is a legally protected wine region. Rules fix which grapes may be grown and how, which is what makes a village name a guarantee of style. Reds here are mostly blends of Grenache, Syrah and Mourvèdre.

The village is known for its stone lanes, its winegrowers' cellars and a market. Vaison-la-Romaine is a short drive to the north.

Vaison-la-Romaine

Events 10–11 Oct

The Provence Guide lists local events in Vaison-la-Romaine for this weekend, 10 and 11 October. Check the guide for times and venues before travelling.

If you're new to this

Vaison-la-Romaine is a small town on the river Ouvèze with two historic halves. The lower town holds some of the largest Roman excavations in France, at the Puymin and La Villasse quarters, with houses, mosaics, a theatre and a museum. Across the river, the medieval upper town (Haute-Ville) climbs to a ruined castle.

The two sides are joined by a single-arched Roman bridge. In September 1992 a catastrophic flood killed dozens of people and wrecked much of the lower town, but the Roman bridge held.

The town hosts a summer festival, Choralies, every third year, and weekly markets. It is a good base for walking in the Dentelles.

Orange

Not verified today

No reliable current news for Orange today, so this is a background note.

If you're new to this

Orange is a town in the Vaucluse with two of the best-preserved Roman monuments in Europe, both on the UNESCO World Heritage list. The Roman theatre has an intact stage wall about 37 metres high, which is rare because most ancient theatres lost theirs. It still hosts opera and concerts, notably the Chorégies d'Orange festival each summer.

The Arc de Triomphe of Orange stands on the old road to Lyon. It is a triple-arched monument from the early Roman Empire, carved with scenes of battles and spoils.

The town's name is older than the fruit. It comes from the Roman Arausio. The Principality of Orange later passed to the House of Nassau, which is how the Dutch royal family got the name "Orange".

Avignon

Opera

Provence Guide listings point to Verdi's opera Aïda rehearsing in Avignon, and to the Fête de la Véraison at nearby Châteauneuf-de-Gadagne. I could not verify performance dates, so check before you go.

If you're new to this

Avignon sits on the Rhône and was the seat of the Catholic popes from 1309 to 1377, when the papacy moved from Rome. The huge Gothic Palais des Papes is the legacy, and the old city's ramparts are largely intact. Both are UNESCO listed.

The Pont Saint-Bénézet, the bridge of the song "Sur le pont d'Avignon", was built in the 12th century. Repeated floods took out most of its arches, so it stops partway across the river.

Each July the city hosts the Festival d'Avignon, one of the biggest theatre festivals in the world, with a huge fringe called the Off.

Aïda is Verdi's opera set in ancient Egypt, premiered in Cairo in 1871. It tells of an enslaved Ethiopian princess who falls in love with an Egyptian commander. It is famous for the Triumphal March.

If you're new to this

Two teams of 15 play two 40-minute halves. You can run with the ball or kick it, but you can only pass it sideways or backwards. A try, where a player grounds the ball over the opponents' line, scores 5 points. A conversion kick adds 2, and a penalty kick or drop goal scores 3.

The game is built on set pieces. A scrum restarts play after minor infringements, with the forwards pushing against each other. A lineout restarts play after the ball goes out of play, with a jumper lifted to catch the throw.

If you're new to this

Cricket is a bat-and-ball game between two teams of 11. One side bats and tries to score runs, the other bowls and fields and tries to get ten batters out. A batter scores by running between the two sets of stumps or by hitting the ball over the boundary for 4 (along the ground) or 6 (in the air).

Formats vary in length. A Test match lasts up to five days with two innings per side and is regarded as the highest form. A one-day international is 50 overs a side (an over is six balls). Twenty20 is 20 overs a side and over in about three hours.

White-ball means the limited-overs formats, which use a white ball and coloured kit. The Ashes between England and Australia is the best-known Test rivalry.

Formula 1 is a world championship for single-seater racing cars. Each season has around two dozen race weekends across the world, called Grands Prix. Ten teams used to compete, and the 2026 grid has 11, with Audi and Cadillac among the newer names.

Drivers score points in each race: 25 for a win, then 18, 15, 12, 10, 8, 6, 4, 2 and 1 for places two to ten. Some weekends also include a shorter sprint race with a smaller points scale. Points add up across the season, and the driver with the most is world champion.

The sport changed its rules for 2026, with new engines and chassis regulations, which is one reason the order at the front looks different from recent years.

Field hockey is played on a pitch by two teams of 11. Players use a curved stick with a flat face on one side and can only hit the ball with that flat side. Goals count only if the ball is struck from inside the shooting circle.

The Bank of Canada's policy rate is 2.25% and its next decision is on 28 October. Inflation was 3.0% in August; September's CPI is due on 19 October. The TSX hit a record near 36,660 in August.

Rate decisions and the CPI release are the two dates that most often move Canadian bond and share prices over the next few weeks.

  • Policy rate: 2.25%, next decision 28 Oct
  • CPI inflation: 3.0% (August); September CPI on 19 Oct
  • TSX composite: record near 36,660 in August

If you're new to this

The Bank of Canada sets its overnight policy rate, the rate that banks charge each other, and uses it to steer inflation toward 2%. Commercial banks set their prime rate from it, which drives variable mortgages and lines of credit.

The Consumer Price Index (CPI) measures how much a typical basket of goods and services costs, compared with a year earlier. When inflation is above target, the bank is less likely to cut rates.

The S&P/TSX Composite is the main Canadian share index. It is heavy in banks, energy and mining, so it rises and falls with commodity prices and financial-sector earnings, and is less weighted to technology than US indexes.

The Canadian dollar also matters. When oil rises it often strengthens, which changes the value of any US or international assets you hold.

United States: rates rising again

Fed hiked 16 Sep

The Federal Reserve raised its target range to 3.75–4.00% on 16 September, with Chair Warsh signalling more increases. That is a shift from the earlier easing story, and markets have had to reprice.

US shares and bond yields move first and global markets follow.

  • Fed target range: 3.75–4.00% (raised 16 Sep)
  • Chair: Warsh; further hikes signalled

If you're new to this

The Federal Reserve is the US central bank. Its policy committee sets a target range for the federal funds rate, and that rate anchors borrowing costs worldwide because the US dollar is the world's main reserve currency.

When the Fed raises rates, newly issued bonds pay more, so existing bonds with lower rates fall in price. High rates also make future profits worth less today, which hurts fast-growing technology shares most.

Because the S&P 500 and Nasdaq are such large parts of global equity funds, a change in US policy can move your ETF even if you hold no US-specific fund.

United Kingdom and France

Europe

The UK holds Bank Rate at 3.75% with CPI at 3.1%, and France is trying to cut a 5.4% deficit with 121.7% debt and a 10-year yield near 4.94%. Both are tied by high borrowing costs.

The UK's next rate decision is on 5 November. France's budget fight is in progress.

If you're new to this

Both countries have high debt and slow growth. In a high-debt country, rising interest rates make the interest bill larger every year, which leaves less for services or tax cuts.

France does not set its own rates. As a euro member it follows the European Central Bank, so it cannot cut rates to help itself and must instead cut spending or raise taxes.

The UK has its own currency, so the pound can fall when investors worry. That raises import prices and inflation, another reason the Bank of England is cautious.

China: slower growth

Q2 GDP 4.3%

China's economy grew 4.3% in the second quarter, and the People's Bank of China has kept policy accommodative to support growth.

That growth is lower than the roughly 8% to 10% rates of earlier decades, and a long property downturn has weighed on households.

If you're new to this

GDP (gross domestic product) is the total value of goods and services produced. Growth of 4.3% is strong for a large developed economy but slow for China, which depends on growth to employ millions of people.

China is the world's largest manufacturer and a major buyer of oil, iron ore and copper. When its growth slows, commodity prices and Canadian resource shares often weaken.

Chinese shares are in most global and emerging-market ETFs but at a modest weight. They are subject to political risk, since regulators can change rules suddenly.

Registered accounts: TFSA, RRSP and FHSA

Canada

The account you hold an ETF in can matter as much as which ETF you pick, because it decides how the growth and income are taxed.

If you're new to this

A Tax-Free Savings Account (TFSA) lets investments grow and be withdrawn tax-free. You contribute from money that has already been taxed. Withdrawn room comes back the following year.

A Registered Retirement Savings Plan (RRSP) gives you a tax deduction when you contribute, and growth is untaxed until you withdraw, when it counts as income. It tends to suit people whose tax rate now is higher than it will be in retirement.

The First Home Savings Account (FHSA) combines both: a deduction going in and tax-free withdrawals if used for a first home. It only suits people who qualify as first-time buyers.

Contribution limits change each year and unused room carries forward, so check the Canada Revenue Agency for yours. A non-registered account has no limit but is taxed on dividends, interest and gains. This is general information, so confirm your position with a tax professional.

All-in-one ETFs, fees and asset mix

Core ideas

An all-in-one ETF holds a mix of stocks and bonds in a single fund and rebalances itself. It is a popular way to keep an investing plan simple.

If you're new to this

An ETF (exchange-traded fund) is a basket of investments that trades on the stock exchange like a single share. An index ETF simply copies a market index, such as the S&P 500, without anyone picking stocks.

The management expense ratio (MER) is the yearly fee taken from the fund, shown as a percentage. All-in-one ETFs usually charge roughly 0.2% to 0.25%. Fees compound against you, so a 1% fund costs thousands more over decades than a 0.2% fund.

Asset allocation is the split between stocks (higher growth, bigger swings) and bonds (steadier, lower return). All-in-one funds come in conservative, balanced, growth and all-equity versions. A person at 45 typically has about 20 years before drawing on retirement money, but how much risk suits you depends on your goals and temperament.

Rebalancing means selling a bit of what has grown and buying what lagged, to return to the target split. An all-in-one does this inside the fund.

Rates, bonds, currency and withholding tax

Core ideas

Four mechanics that quietly affect returns: how bond prices react to interest rates, currency movements, hedging, and the tax withheld on foreign dividends.

If you're new to this

Bond prices and yields move in opposite directions. If rates rise, newly issued bonds pay more, so older bonds fall in price, and longer-dated bonds fall further. A bond ETF can therefore lose value while rates rise, even though the bonds will pay back at maturity.

When a Canadian holds US or international shares, returns depend on the share price and on the exchange rate. If the US dollar falls against the Canadian dollar, a US fund can lose value in Canadian terms. A hedged fund removes most of that effect for a small cost, while an unhedged fund leaves it in.

Foreign governments often withhold tax on dividends paid to non-residents, typically 15% for US dividends under the tax treaty. How much you pay depends on the account (an RRSP is treated more favourably for US-listed funds than a TFSA) and whether the fund is listed in Canada or the US, so it is worth reading the fund's documents.

None of this is a recommendation, and the right answer depends on your situation. A fee-only financial planner can check your specifics.
